# Staying T-Shaped

> **Core skill:** Building depth without burning your breadth

## What Changes from Junior to Mid

A junior engineer accumulates breadth by necessity: every task is new. A mid-level engineer faces the opposite pressure: the temptation to drop everything except the one thing they are good at. Staying T-shaped is the discipline of doing both on purpose: a broad working competence across the shared capabilities of software engineering, and one depth bar deep enough to be trusted with hard problems. This note is about keeping the breadth alive while the depth compounds.

| Aspect | Junior | Mid |
|--------|--------|-----|
| Breadth | Gained incidentally, task by task | Kept deliberately, through habits that cost real time |
| Depth | None yet; everything is shallow | One bar deepening through projects, evidence, and repetition |
| Learning | Whatever the sprint demands | A plan: depth first, breadth on a schedule |
| Identity | "I do what the team needs" | "I am the person for X, and I stay usable for the rest" |
| Assignment choice | Takes whatever comes | Selects assignments that deepen the bar without eroding the base |
| Career risk | Being generic | Being narrow just as the domain shifts under you |
| Checkpoints | Never | Reviews shape at promotions, role changes, and yearly |

## Core Frameworks

### The Anatomy of the T

| Part | What It Means | What It Buys | What It Costs | Failure Mode |
|------|---------------|--------------|---------------|--------------|
| Breadth bar | Working competence in problem framing, design, testing, operations, and communication | Adaptability, credible collaboration, options when domains shift | Ongoing time spent outside your specialty | Dilettantism: shallow at everything, trusted with nothing |
| Depth bar | Real expertise in one chosen area: the work you are trusted to do when it is hard | Distinctiveness, leverage, the right to hard problems | Deliberate practice and repeated exposure over years | The one-trick expert whose trick stops being needed |
| The join | Enough depth in the specialty to see how it touches the rest | Translation between specialists; the ability to lead across areas | Judgment about when to stop deepening | The bridge role nobody values until it is missing |

### Keeping Breadth Alive

| Habit | Cadence | Real Cost | Signal It Works |
|-------|---------|-----------|-----------------|
| Rotation into adjacent work | One rotation per year: ops, testing, data, or a customer-facing stint | Weeks of lower productivity while you ramp | You can hold a real conversation with that specialty's owners |
| Reading outside your lane | A few hours a month on other teams' postmortems, designs, and docs | Time taken from depth work | You notice cross-team problems before they reach you |
| Pairing across specialties | One pairing session per month with an engineer outside your area | Scheduling and context cost | You learn their vocabulary and stop making naive assumptions |
| Teaching the base | Occasional review or onboarding outside your specialty | Interruption to deep work | Your explanations survive contact with real questions |
| Whole-system ownership slices | Touch one full vertical slice per quarter: design, build, test, deploy, monitor | Slower than staying in your lane | You can debug across the boundaries others avoid |

### When to Widen and When to Deepen

| Signal | Widen | Deepen |
|--------|-------|--------|
| Your specialty is shrinking or automating away | Yes: build the adjacent bar before you need it | No: doubling down narrows your options |
| You are the only person who can do X | Keep enough breadth to teach and delegate X | Yes: but pair it with a plan to spread the skill |
| Every interesting problem is being handed to someone deeper | Widen only what blocks your depth | Yes: deliberate practice is the bottleneck now |
| You cannot follow adjacent teams' design discussions | Yes: the breadth gap is now costing you influence | Not yet |
| You have been the expert in one area for several years and growth has stalled | Adjacent depth creates new problems and new options | A lateral deepening into a neighbor, not the same hole |
| You are early in your career and all bars are shallow | Breadth first: the base is the foundation of the T | One bar beginning to deepen, not five |

## In Practice

**Depth is chosen from evidence, not from fashion.** The depth bar should come from what your fit tests showed: the work you return to, the problems you finish, the feedback that says you see things others miss. Picking a depth for market heat alone produces a bar you will not be willing to grind when the work gets unglamorous. Use the leaning notes in this area as the input; the depth decision is their output.

**Depth compounds only through deliberate repetition.** A deep bar is built by doing the hard version of the work repeatedly: the ambiguous design, the nasty production bug, the evaluation nobody wants to build. Comfortable repetition of easy work builds tenure, not depth. Seek the version of the task that is just beyond your current reach and leave artifacts (write-ups, tools, talks) that prove the work happened.

**Breadth has to be scheduled or it dies.** When deadlines press, breadth is the first thing dropped and the last thing noticed missing. Assign a cadence: one pairing session per month, one adjacent-team document per week, one rotation per year. The cost is real and worth it, because a breadth habit is what keeps you employable when the domain shifts.

**Widen when your narrowness blocks the work; deepen when it no longer does.** The tests are practical: can you follow an adjacent team's design review, can you debug across a boundary you did not build, can you explain the system beyond your slice? If the answer is no and it costs you, widen. If every interesting problem still demands more depth than you have, deepen.

**The T is allowed to move.** Career checkpoints (promotion, role change, a new company, a yearly review) are the natural moments to re-measure your shape. A depth bar that served you at mid-level may not be the one the next five years need, and switching depth mid-career is normal when the evidence says so. What is expensive is switching every year; what is fatal is never measuring.

**Teaching is how the two halves integrate.** When you teach your specialty to generalists or your broad base to newcomers, you find the gaps in both. Engineers who teach regularly maintain breadth almost for free and sharpen the join between their bars. If you cannot explain your specialty to a mid-level engineer in an hour, that is the next learning target.

## Practical Exercise

Measure and shape your T over one quarter:

1. Draw your current T honestly: rate your breadth across problem framing, design, testing, operations, and communication; name one depth bar and rate it against people you respect.
2. Choose your depth bar from evidence: cite the fit tests and past work that show you return to this area and improve at it.
3. Write a deliberate practice plan for the bar: three hard tasks, at the edge of your ability, each leaving an artifact.
4. Schedule one breadth habit with a real cadence: pairing, reading adjacent designs, or a rotation; put it on the calendar.
5. Take one whole-system slice end to end (design through monitoring), and note every boundary where your knowledge thinned.
6. Teach one concept from your specialty to a generalist audience and collect where the explanations broke.
7. Review the quarter at a checkpoint: widen, deepen, or shift the bar, and record the rule you will use next time.

## Practical Applications

- [ ] My depth bar is named, chosen from evidence, and has three hard tasks planned
- [ ] At least one breadth habit runs on a fixed cadence on my calendar
- [ ] My assignment choices over the last quarter deepened the bar without eroding the base
- [ ] I can follow adjacent specialties' design discussions without a translator
- [ ] I have taught my specialty to someone outside it in the last six months
- [ ] My depth work leaves artifacts: write-ups, tools, reviews, or talks
- [ ] A shape review is scheduled at my next career checkpoint
- [ ] My rule for widen-versus-deepen is written down, not improvised

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Fails | Better Approach |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Specializing overnight | Fragile skills and shrinking options with no evidence of fit | Specialize through tested evidence, one bar at a time |
| Keeping breadth as an intention | Unscheduled breadth evaporates under deadline pressure | Give breadth a cadence and a calendar slot |
| Depth through easy repetition | Tenure in a tool is not expertise in a domain | Seek the hard version of the work and leave artifacts |
| Refusing to shift the bar | The domain moves; a sunk-cost specialty stops paying | Re-measure the shape at every career checkpoint |
| Five shallow bars instead of one deep | Nothing you are trusted with when it is hard | One bar first; adjacent depth only when the first compounds |
| Dropping breadth to look senior | Senior scope requires translating across areas | Depth earns attention; breadth earns scope |

## Success Indicators

- Your depth bar is named, evidenced, and getting harder work every quarter
- Your breadth habits run on schedule even during busy months
- Adjacent teams treat you as a credible conversation partner
- You can name the moment your narrowness last cost you, and what you did about it
- Your artifacts show deliberate practice, not just time served
- Your last shape review produced a written decision: widen, deepen, or shift
