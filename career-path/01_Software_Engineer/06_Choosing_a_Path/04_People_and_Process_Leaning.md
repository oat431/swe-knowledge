# People and Process Leaning

> **Core skill:** Testing fit for tech lead, management, and program work

## What Changes from Junior to Mid

A junior engineer measures a good week in code written. A mid-level engineer who leans toward people and process starts noticing that the team's output matters more than their own: the review that unblocked a teammate, the plan that kept a project honest, the conversation that turned a conflict into a decision. This note helps you test whether leading, managing, or coordinating (Tech Lead, Engineering Manager, Technical Program Manager, Project and Program Manager) is the work that gives you energy.

| Aspect | Junior | Mid |
|--------|--------|-----|
| Team output | Optimizes own tasks | Optimizes the team's flow and the outcomes it delivers |
| Meetings | Endures them | Shapes them: agenda, decision, owner, follow-up |
| Feedback | Avoids giving it | Gives specific, timely feedback and asks for it |
| Conflict | Escalates or withdraws | Facilitates the disagreement toward a decision |
| Planning | Commits to a sprint | Runs a plan with dependencies, risks, and honest status |
| Credit | Wants recognition for own work | Measures success by the team's results and growth |
| Direction | Waits for clarity | Creates enough clarity for others to move |

## Core Frameworks

### Leading, Managing, and Coordinating: What Each Role Does

| Path | The Actual Job | Signature Artifacts | Judged On |
|------|----------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Tech Lead | Holds one team's technical coherence while still delivering; sets direction, reviews design, unblocks, and balances team needs against own work | Technical direction, design decisions, team working agreements | Whether the team delivers a coherent technical result |
| Engineering Manager | Creates the conditions for a team to deliver and grow; hiring, feedback, performance, team health, and context from the organization | Hiring plans, one-to-one notes, performance reviews, team plans | Team health, delivery, retention, and growth of engineers |
| Technical Program Manager | Coordinates complex technical initiatives across teams; surfaces dependencies, risks, and decisions across organizational boundaries | Program plans, risk registers, status rollups, decision logs | Predictability and risk control across multiple teams |
| Project and Program Manager | Manages coordinated work toward agreed outcomes; scope, schedule, stakeholders, and delivery process | Project plans, schedules, stakeholder updates, retrospectives | Outcomes delivered against commitments, with controlled change |

### The Forks You Will Actually Face

| Fork | The Question | Leaning One Way | Leaning the Other |
|------|--------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| Tech Lead versus Engineering Manager | Do I want to own the technical result or the team's conditions? | Stay TL if design and code keep you energized | Move EM if growth, hiring, and team health are the real reward |
| IC versus manager | Is my craft the software or the organization around it? | IC scope grows through influence and depth | Management grows through people systems and judgment at scale |
| Leadership versus coordination | Do I want to set direction or make complex delivery predictable? | TL or EM if direction-setting drives you | TPM or PM if alignment and planning are the craft |
| Formal versus informal | Do I need the title or the responsibility? | Take formal scope when accountability matches authority | Lead informally first as a low-cost fit test |

### Energizing Versus Draining Signals and Fit Tests

| Element | Energizing If | Draining If | Low-Cost Fit Test |
|---------|---------------|-------------|-------------------|
| Meetings and alignment | Shared context and decisions in conversation feel productive | Every meeting feels like lost building time | Run one meeting with an agenda and a forced decision |
| Feedback and growth | Someone's improvement after your feedback is the reward | Feedback conversations feel like conflict to survive | Give one specific behavior-level feedback and ask for some back |
| Conflict | Untangling a disagreement to a decision absorbs you | Emotional tension lingers and wrecks your day | Facilitate one live disagreement to a written decision |
| Planning and process | Honest plans and clean process make work calm | Repeating process for its own sake bores you | Own one project's plan and status for a full cycle |
| Context switching | Fragmented days still feel like progress | Interruption destroys your focus for hours | Track your week in interruptions and how it felt |
| Accountability without control | Responsibility for outcomes you cannot command is motivating | Being accountable without authority feels unfair | Own a cross-team outcome for one release |

## In Practice

**Separate leading from managing before you choose.** A Tech Lead owns a team's technical result while still building; an Engineering Manager owns the team's conditions and the growth of its people. Many engineers want "the lead thing" and are drained by performance conversations, hiring, and one-to-ones; others discover that a teammate's promotion is a bigger win than any design they shipped. The fork is about which feedback loop rewards you.

**Program and project work is a craft of its own, not diluted engineering.** TPMs and project managers make complex delivery predictable: dependencies surfaced early, risks named before they bite, decisions logged, status honest even when it is bad news. If building and protecting a plan across teams feels like meaningful work, this path uses engineering judgment in a different medium; if it feels like herding, it will drain you permanently.

**The IC-to-manager switch changes your unit of work.** As an IC you finish tasks; as a manager your output is the team's results and your leverage is context, feedback, and staffing. The switch costs you daily building and hands you responsibility without direct control. Test it while the exit is cheap: take an interim lead role, mentorship duties, or a project lead seat before resigning from the craft.

**Meetings are the raw material of this family.** People and process roles spend their days in conversation: planning, feedback, alignment, conflict, and status. The question is not how to avoid meetings but whether the outcomes (a decision made, a person unblocked, a plan made honest) compensate you for the interruption. Track a week of your conversations and judge the exchange rate honestly.

**Feedback skill is the admission ticket.** Every role in this family runs on telling people specific things about their work and behavior, and hearing the same back, without drama. If you have never given behavior-level feedback, that is your first fit test: timely, specific, kind, and about something changeable. How that conversation feels is one of the strongest signals you can collect.

**Conflicts and bad news are the hard core of the job.** Managers hold performance problems, program managers surface slipped dates, tech leads say no to popular designs. Avoidance in any of these produces worse outcomes later and burns trust. If the prospect of delivering a hard message sits heavily on you in a way that practice does not relieve, weigh that cost before committing.

## Practical Exercise

Test the people and process direction over one quarter:

1. Write three sentences on the most rewarding time you helped another person or a plan succeed, and name what the reward actually was.
2. Mentor one engineer through a real piece of work: frame the goal, coach rather than take over, and ask for feedback on your mentoring at the end.
3. Own one project's plan for a full cycle: scope, dependencies, risks, weekly status, and one honest bad-news message.
4. Own one process for a month (a review ritual, a release checklist, a retro), and change one thing about it based on evidence.
5. Give one piece of specific behavior-level feedback and request feedback on yourself in the same conversation.
6. Facilitate one disagreement to a written decision and note how the tension felt afterwards.
7. Review the quarter's log of energizing and draining moments and pick which fork (lead, manage, or coordinate) the evidence supports.

## Practical Applications

- [ ] I can explain the difference between Tech Lead, Engineering Manager, TPM, and project manager in terms of daily work
- [ ] I have mentored someone end to end and asked them how it went
- [ ] I have run a project plan with real dependencies and delivered one hard status message
- [ ] I give behavior-level feedback and actively request it back
- [ ] I know how I respond to conflict and whether facilitation suits me
- [ ] My week in interruptions is measured, and I know the cost honestly
- [ ] I have named which fork I am leaning toward and the evidence for it
- [ ] My next step is a low-cost test, not a permanent role change

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Fails | Better Approach |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Choosing management to escape bad code work | You trade code problems for people problems, which are harder | Move toward what pulls you, not away from what pushes you |
| Assuming the Tech Lead is the manager | The roles own different outcomes and reward different work | Test both flavors before accepting either title |
| Taking scope without authority deliberately | Chronic accountability without control burns out fast | Negotiate the mandate before accepting the responsibility |
| Judging by one good mentoring experience | Mentoring is the pleasant subset of people work | Test feedback, conflict, and planning before concluding |
| Treating process as beneath engineering | Coordination work is what makes multi-team delivery possible | Judge the craft by its outcomes, not by code volume |
| Waiting until the promotion to practice | The role is earned by doing its work first | Run the low-cost tests now, inside your current job |

## Success Indicators

- You can describe each of the four roles by its daily work, not its title
- At least one mentoring, project, or process test is complete with honest feedback
- You deliver hard messages without avoidance or drama
- Your energy log shows which of leading, managing, and coordinating rewards you
- You know your interruption tolerance and have priced it into the choice
- Your next move is a reversible test, not a one-way commitment
