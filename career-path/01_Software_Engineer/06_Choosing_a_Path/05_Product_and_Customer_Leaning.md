# Product and Customer Leaning

> **Core skill:** Testing fit for product, field, and advocacy work

## What Changes from Junior to Mid

A junior engineer takes the ticket as given and is glad the code works. A mid-level engineer who leans toward product and customers starts asking why this ticket exists, who the user is, and whether the built thing actually solved their problem. This note helps you test whether the product-and-customer family — Product Manager, Forward Deployed Engineer, Developer Advocate and Technical Consultant, Solutions and Enterprise Architect — is where your energy goes: toward customer contact, communication, and the messy gap between what people say and what they need.

| Aspect | Junior | Mid |
|--------|--------|-----|
| Requirements | Accepts the specification | Joins discovery and questions the problem behind it |
| Customer contact | Rare and scripted | Regular and direct: calls, visits, support threads, community |
| Communication | Explains the code | Explains the value, the trade-off, and the why, to non-engineers |
| Ambiguity | Requests clearer tickets | Works inside fuzzy needs until they become buildable |
| Success | Features shipped | Problems resolved and value delivered and measured |
| Exposure | Internal only | External: users, customers, audiences, sometimes travel |
| Scope | One team's backlog | A product line, a customer's environment, a community, or a market |

## Core Frameworks

### What the Work Actually Looks Like Day to Day

| Path | A Normal Week | Signature Artifacts | Judged On |
|------|---------------|---------------------|-----------|
| Product Manager | Discovery calls, backlog and prioritization, writing problem statements and specs, analyzing usage, aligning engineering and stakeholders | Problem briefs, roadmaps, specs, outcome metrics | Whether the right problems get solved and the outcomes land |
| Forward Deployed Engineer | Working inside the customer's environment: integrating, adapting, debugging in the field, feeding patterns back to the product | Integrations, custom solutions, field feedback reports | Whether technology works in the customer's real conditions |
| Developer Advocate or Technical Consultant | Creating guides and talks, answering users, running community, demoing features, representing user pain internally | Tutorials, talks, demos, sample code, community threads | User success, adoption, and the credibility of the voice you carry |
| Solutions or Enterprise Architect | Pre-sales and post-sales design, understanding business context, sizing solutions, aligning technical and enterprise change | Solution designs, proposals, reference architectures | Whether the proposed technology fits the customer's real problem |

### Energizing Versus Draining Signals

| Element | Energizing If | Draining If |
|---------|---------------|-------------|
| Customer contact | Real users and their messy problems are the interesting part | Repetitive support and emotional customers wear you down |
| Travel and field time | Being on site, in the room, in the environment suits you | Unpredictable travel and time away from home costs too much |
| Ambiguity | Fuzzy needs are raw material you shape into decisions | You want well-defined problems with clear success criteria |
| External exposure | Talks, demos, and public writing give energy | Being on stage or visible online feels threatening |
| Persuasion and negotiation | Winning commitment across customers and internal teams is a craft | Repeated persuasion without authority feels political |
| Breadth over depth | Learning a customer's domain one week and a new tool the next is fun | Shallow, wide work feels like never mastering anything |

### Fit Tests You Can Run Inside Your Current Job

| Test | How to Run It | Evidence For | Evidence Against |
|------|---------------|--------------|------------------|
| Run a discovery conversation | Interview one real user about their problem; write what they need, not what they asked for | The listening and reframing absorb you | The conversation feels like pulling teeth |
| Write a guide | Document a feature or workflow so a stranger can succeed with it | Teaching through writing is satisfying | The rewrite for clarity feels like drudgery |
| Demo a feature | Present a feature to an audience and handle live questions | The performance and questions give energy | You dread the exposure and the improvisation |
| Sit in on a customer call | Join a support, success, or pre-sales call and debrief afterward | The translation between sides fascinates you | The sales flavor or repetition puts you off |
| Own an outcome metric | Take one feature and measure whether it solved the user's problem | Outcome thinking, not output, motivates you | Shipping and moving on is what you prefer |

## In Practice

**The common thread of this family is customer contact plus communication.** All four roles live in the gap between a person with a problem and a technical system that might solve it: discovering what is needed, delivering it in the customer's environment, teaching a community, or designing a solution before the deal. If talking to real users and translating across contexts is the part of engineering you would do more of, this family matches; if it is the part you tolerate, it will dominate your week and exhaust you.

**Product management is problem selection, not project management.** The product manager's craft is deciding which problem deserves the team and proving it was solved: discovery interviews, prioritization under scarcity, crisp problem statements, and honest outcome metrics. Engineers often move toward it because they are tired of building the wrong thing — but the daily work is writing, alignment, and stakeholder negotiation, not design elegance. Test the taste of that work with a real discovery conversation before you commit.

**Field and solutions work trades depth for immediacy.** Forward Deployed Engineers and Solutions Architects see their code or design work in the customer's own environment, under real constraints, often with travel and hard deadlines. The reward is visible, direct impact and rapid learning across domains; the cost is shallow tooling depth, unpredictable schedules, and emotional load when the customer is unhappy. Weigh the schedule and travel terms of the specific role, not the romance of "being close to the customer."

**Advocacy is teaching under public exposure.** Developer advocates and technical consultants write, speak, demo, and carry user pain back into the product. The work rewards generosity and clarity, and it puts you on stage, in public threads, and in front of audiences regularly. If public writing and live demos give energy, the path compounds fast; if exposure costs you days of dread, no amount of product love compensates.

**Ambiguity is the standing condition here.** Customer problems arrive fuzzy, requirements change mid-flight, and success may be defined only after delivery. Every role in this family requires shaping unclear needs into decisions and being comfortable revising them. If your best work happens inside tight specifications, the product-and-customer family will feel like permanent improvisation.

**Run the cheap tests first, and include the cost side.** A discovery conversation, a written guide, or one demo takes days, not years, and tells you far more than a job title does. Log not only whether the activity energized you but what it cost: prep anxiety, travel fatigue, recovery time. Fit is the exchange rate between energy and cost, and this family's costs are unusually personal.

## Practical Exercise

Test the product and customer direction over one quarter:

1. Write three sentences on the most rewarding contact you ever had with a user or customer, and what exactly rewarded you.
2. Run one discovery conversation with a real user: ask about their problem, not their feature request; write up needs versus asks.
3. Write one guide that lets a stranger succeed with a feature you built; note how the simplification felt.
4. Demo one feature to an audience of at least three people and handle live questions; record the exposure cost afterwards.
5. Join one customer, support, or pre-sales call and debrief: what translation between the two sides was needed?
6. Measure one outcome for a feature you shipped: did it solve the user's problem, and how do you know?
7. Write the verdict: which of the four roles matches your energy and cost profile, and what test would confirm it.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Fails | Better Approach |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Choosing product work to escape coding | The daily work is writing, alignment, and negotiation | Test discovery and stakeholder work before switching |
| Falling for the field's romance | The impact is real, but so are travel, on-call hours, and unhappy customers | Price the schedule and emotional load of the specific role |
| Confusing advocacy with marketing | Credibility comes from honest technical teaching, not promotion | Judge the work by user success, not by audience size |
| Ignoring the ambiguity cost | Constant fuzzy problems exhaust engineers who thrive on precision | Sample discovery work honestly before committing |
| Judging fit on one great interaction | A single demo or visit flatters the role | Collect evidence across discovery, writing, and exposure |
| Dropping technical depth entirely | These roles lose credibility fast without real engineering judgment | Keep a technical floor alongside customer-facing practice |

## Success Indicators

- You can explain what each of the four roles does on a normal Tuesday
- At least two customer-facing tests are complete with cost logs, not just highlights
- You separate what users ask for from what they need in your own writing
- You know your exposure and travel tolerance in concrete terms
- Your choice names both the energy source and the standing cost
- Your next move is a cheap test inside your current job
