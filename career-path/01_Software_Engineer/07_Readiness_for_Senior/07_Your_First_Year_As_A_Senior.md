# Your First Year As A Senior

> **Core skill:** Knowing what to expect when the level actually arrives

## What Changes from Junior to Mid

A junior engineer builds confidence by closing tasks with guidance. A mid-level engineer builds a reputation for reliable delivery: features land, surprises are surfaced early, and the quality of their work is trusted. That reputation is what earns the next level.

The promotion itself is an expectation reset, not a finishing line. The work that earned the title (dependable execution on well-defined scope) is now the baseline, and the new job is the ambiguity, judgment, and team lift that surrounded it. The first year is where the gap between "was operating at senior scope sometimes" and "is consistently trusted with senior scope" gets closed.

| Aspect | Junior | Mid |
|--------|--------|-----|
| Success measure | Task done well | Feature delivered and verified |
| Guidance | Frequent, step-level | Weekly, goal-level |
| Ambiguity | Escalated upward | Reduced within the task |
| Reputation | Reliable on assignments | Reliable on a feature end to end |
| Failure mode | Slow or incorrect execution | Good execution of the wrong framing |
| Next step | Learn the craft | Learn to choose and frame the work |

At senior, the measure changes again: the area's health and direction, and whether the people around you are more effective because you are there.

## Core Frameworks

### What Changes and What Does Not

| Dimension | Changes at senior | Stays the same |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------|
| Scope | You are accountable for an area, not only a feature | You still deliver concrete work |
| Expectations | Framing, judgment, and team lift are now the baseline | Quality and reliability still matter |
| Autonomy | You are expected to decide, not to ask first | You still escalate genuine judgment calls |
| Feedback | Less correction, more challenge | You still need to ask for it |
| Relationships | Peers expect you to unblock and review | You still need help from others |
| Visibility | Your reasoning is scrutinized, not just your output | Your output still has to be correct |
| Definition of done | The area is healthier, not just the ticket closed | Work still ships and is verified |

### The First Ninety Days

| Phase | Focus | Concrete output |
|-------|-------|-----------------|
| Days 1 to 30 | Learn the area's history and constraints | A written map of components, owners, failure modes, and open risks |
| Days 1 to 30 | Agree the expectations with your manager | A short list of what success looks like in six months |
| Days 31 to 60 | Set your operating rhythm | A weekly review of backlog, risks, and decisions that others can see |
| Days 31 to 60 | Pick one visible improvement | A scoped fix to a known pain, with a before-and-after |
| Days 61 to 90 | Deliver the improvement and document it | A shipped change plus an ADR or runbook update |
| Days 61 to 90 | Review the expectations again | A revised list of focus areas and one thing you will stop doing |

### Your Operating Rhythm

| Cadence | Activity | Purpose |
|---------|----------|---------|
| Daily | Triage the area's alerts and incoming requests | Keep small problems from becoming incidents |
| Weekly | Review the backlog, open risks, and decisions made | Make ownership visible and catch drift |
| Weekly | One written note to the team: changed, at risk, needed | Replace status questions with a durable record |
| Biweekly | 1:1 with your manager on scope and expectations | Calibrate before the quarter ends, not after |
| Monthly | Read the area's incidents and near-misses | Find the recurring failure worth fixing |
| Quarterly | Review evidence and the next quarter's focus | Keep growth deliberate rather than accidental |

## In Practice

**Expect the reset, and do not read it as regression.** The first months at a new level often feel like being a beginner again: the questions are bigger, the feedback is about judgment rather than correctness, and the scoreboard is less legible. That discomfort is the normal signal that the scope changed, not evidence that the promotion was a mistake.

**Resist the urge to prove yourself with volume.** New seniors often take on too many areas, too many reviews, and too many meetings to demonstrate value. Overcommitment produces shallow attention and missed incidents, which damages trust faster than a smaller scope delivered well. Choose depth in one area before breadth, and keep the habits that earned the title (verifying your own changes, asking for review, flagging risk early), because they scale with your scope rather than becoming optional.

**Learn the area before you improve it.** Read the incidents, the design docs, the open risks, and the tickets that were closed as "won't fix". The history tells you which constraints are real and which are habits. Improvements proposed without that context tend to solve a problem the team already solved, or revive one they deliberately buried.

**Agree the new scorecard explicitly.** Ask your manager what would make them say "this is going well" in six months, and write the answer down. The expectations at senior are often implicit, and implicit expectations default to whatever the manager last saw you do. Making them explicit converts an anxious year into a set of checkable goals.

**Set an operating rhythm early and keep it boring.** A weekly note, a groomed backlog, and a monthly incident review do more for your reputation than any single launch. Rhythm is what makes ownership visible when nothing dramatic is happening, and it is the first thing that slips when pressure rises.

**Choose one visible improvement, not a transformation.** Pick a problem the team already agrees is painful, fix it end to end, and document the before and after. One finished improvement establishes your judgment; a half-finished overhaul establishes the opposite. Save the larger changes for when your credibility is established.

**Look ahead without jumping.** Understand the staff-level delta (direction across teams, not just depth in one area), but do not start performing it in your first year. The senior level has its own depth to build: knowing an area well enough to be trusted with its riskiest corners. Use the staff material as orientation, not as the next checklist.

## Practical Exercise

1. In your first month, write a one-page map of your area: components, owners, dependencies, failure modes, and open risks.
2. Ask your manager for their six-month definition of success, and record it verbatim.
3. Write your own list of what you think the role requires, then compare the two lists and discuss the differences.
4. Set a weekly operating rhythm (backlog review, risk note, decisions log) and run it for four weeks before changing it.
5. Identify the one improvement the team already agrees is painful, and scope it to something shippable within a month.
6. Ship it, document the before and after, and add the artifact to your evidence file.
7. At the ninety-day mark, review the expectations with your manager and name one thing you will stop doing.

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Fails | Better Approach |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Trying to prove the promotion immediately | Overreach produces shallow work and missed risk | Deliver one area well before expanding |
| Treating the title as permission to stop learning | The area's depth is still ahead of you | Keep reading incidents and asking questions |
| Taking on every review and meeting | Attention spreads thin and ownership suffers | Protect time for the area you own |
| Waiting for explicit direction | The role expects you to frame and propose | Bring a plan and ask for correction |
| Redesigning the area in month one | The history and constraints are not yet understood | Fix a known pain first; save redesign for later |
| Dropping the mid-level habits | Verification and review are still expected | Keep them; their value scales with your scope |
| Performing staff-level work too early | Direction without depth reads as ambition, not judgment | Build senior depth first; orient, do not perform |

## Success Indicators

- Your manager's six-month definition of success is written and agreed
- You can explain your area's risks and history to a newcomer
- Your weekly rhythm continued through at least one busy period
- One visible improvement shipped and is used by the team
- Peers bring you problems before they become incidents
- You are deepening one area rather than spreading across many
