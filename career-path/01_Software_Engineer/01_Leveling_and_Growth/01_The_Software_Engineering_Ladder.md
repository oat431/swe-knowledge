# The Software Engineering Ladder

> **Core skill:** Reading engineering levels as expectations, not titles

## What Changes from Junior to Mid

A ladder is a company's written statement of what it expects at each level: the scope of work you can carry, the amount of supervision you need, the ambiguity you can absorb, the outcomes you are trusted to produce, and how you communicate around all of it. Moving from junior to mid is not a change of badge; it is crossing from "tasks completed with guidance" to "problems carried to done with light supervision." Titles differ wildly between companies, but those five expectations transfer everywhere.

| Aspect | Junior | Mid |
|--------|--------|-----|
| Unit of work | Well-specified tasks | Features and problem areas |
| Guidance | Regular direction and review | Light supervision, self-directed |
| Ambiguity | Resolved before the task reaches you | You reduce it yourself on the way |
| Escalation | Frequent, expected | Rare, reserved for genuine judgment calls |
| Visibility | Manager knows your work | Team routes questions to your area |
| Failure mode | Blocked waiting for instruction | Surprise: work stalls or shifts without warning |

## Core Frameworks

### The Five Dimensions Every Ladder Measures

| Dimension | What It Measures | Junior Signal | Mid Signal |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|------------|
| Scope | Size of the problem you can hold | One task, one file, one ticket | A feature or small system end to end |
| Autonomy | Supervision you require | Daily direction, step-by-step review | Weekly alignment, review of key decisions |
| Ambiguity | Uncertainty you absorb | Requirements arrive already clarified | You clarify requirements yourself |
| Impact | Outcomes you are trusted to produce | Output: code written and merged | Outcome: behavior changed, metric moved |
| Communication | How work and risk travel | Reports status when asked | Surfaces risk early, writes things down |

### Common Rungs and Where They Sit

| Rung | Scope | Supervision | Parallel Rail |
|------|-------|-------------|---------------|
| Entry / Junior | Tasks within one codebase | High; frequent check-ins | Intern or associate titles |
| Mid / Software Engineer | Features, small services | Light; decision-level review | Senior at some companies, mid at others |
| Senior | Multi-quarter technical problems | Minimal; mentors others | Tech Lead track begins to fork |
| Staff+ | Cross-team technical direction | Self-set; influences org strategy | Management track: people and org scope |
| Management | Team outcomes and people growth | Peer to senior/staff on the technical rail | Not a promotion from senior; a change of job |

### Reading Your Company's Ladder Document

| Ladder Section | What It Actually Tells You | How To Use It |
|----------------|----------------------------|---------------|
| Level summary | The one-paragraph expectation per level | Anchor every self-assessment to this sentence |
| Behavioral anchors | Observable actions that signal a level | Treat as examples, not an exhaustive checklist |
| Scope language ("owns", "drives", "influences") | Verbs encode the autonomy and impact expected | Match your evidence to the verb, not the noun |
| Promotion criteria | The threshold your org actually rewards | Build evidence against these, not against your own theory |
| Anti-examples or failure modes | Where people at this level get stuck | Review before your next project kickoff |

### Title Translation Across Companies

| Your Title Elsewhere | What The Expectation Usually Maps To | Verification Question |
|----------------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------------|
| "Software Engineer I / II" | Junior or mid depending on org size | Can you carry a feature without daily direction? |
| "Software Engineer" | Mid at most product companies | What scope does the ladder grant without a modifier? |
| "Senior Software Engineer" | Mid at large labs; senior at startups | Does "senior" here imply mentoring and multi-quarter scope? |
| "Member of Technical Staff" | Anything from mid to staff | Ask which behavioral anchors apply to the title |
| "Engineer 3 / 4 / 5" | Ladder rung number, org-specific | Ask what the next number requires in evidence |

## In Practice

**Read ladders as behavioral anchors, not as checklists.** Anchor lines describe observable behavior at a level; they are illustrations of the expectation, not a point-scoring system. If you can check every anchor and still get no scope increase, the missing piece is usually judgment in motion: doing the right unlisted thing at the right moment. Study the anchors to learn what your company notices, then work on the underlying behavior.

**Titles are company-local; expectations are portable.** A "senior" title at one company can map to mid-level scope at another, because leveling is calibrated to the org's size, complexity, and pay bands. When you move, carry your evidence of scope and autonomy, not your title. In interviews and leveling conversations, describe what you carried and what decisions you made, and let the counterpart map it to their ladder.

**Tenure is an input, never a level.** Time in role gives you context and codebase familiarity, which makes some behaviors easier to demonstrate. It does not by itself increase the scope you can hold or reduce the supervision you need. Promotions that rest on tenure alone collapse the moment the ladder is enforced; promotions backed by changed behavior hold up anywhere.

**Judge yourself against the next rung's behaviors, not against the fastest person on the team.** Speed of typing and raw output volume are the least differentiating parts of engineering work. The ladder rewards scope, judgment, reliability, and communication; compare your last quarter to those dimensions. A useful question is: which tasks would change ownership if I were operating one level up?

**Use the ladder to make promotion conversations concrete.** Managers cannot argue with a request that cites the document they are calibrating against. Ask "which anchor am I missing for the next rung?" instead of "when do I get promoted?" This turns a political conversation into a working session on specific behaviors and evidence.

**Treat the ladder as a map, not a rail.** A ladder shows the terrain of levels; it does not prescribe one path through it. You can move laterally into a new domain, go deep in one area, or grow in bursts between plateaus. The map's job is orientation: knowing where you are, what is north of you, and which route you are choosing this quarter.

## Practical Exercise

Audit your company's ladder and position yourself on it:
1. Locate your company's ladder document, or pick a public ladder from progression.fyi as a stand-in
2. Extract the five dimensions (scope, autonomy, ambiguity, impact, communication) from its level descriptions
3. List the behavioral anchors at your level and at the next level
4. Gather three pieces of evidence from your last three months of work and match each to an anchor
5. Mark every anchor at the next level as demonstrated, partial, or absent for you today
6. Ask your manager which single anchor they would want to see demonstrated next
7. Write a two-line growth target: the behavior, and where you will practice it in the next month

## Practical Applications

- [ ] Paste your company's five level summaries into your notes and annotate each with one example from your team
- [ ] Rewrite your last project description using the ladder's verbs for your target level
- [ ] Before each 1:1, pick one ladder anchor to discuss with your manager
- [ ] When reading a job description elsewhere, map its seniority claims to expected scope, not to the title word
- [ ] Keep a one-page "where I am on the ladder" note and update it quarterly
- [ ] When you join a new team, ask which behaviors this team rewards at your level

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Fails | Better Approach |
|---------|--------------|-----------------|
| Title-chasing across companies | Titles are calibrated per org; you can "move up" and do the same work | Move toward scope and learning; translate titles through expected behavior |
| Assuming tenure equals level | Time served builds context but not scope or judgment | Measure the last two quarters against behavioral anchors |
| Treating the ladder as a literal checklist | Anchors are examples; gaming them produces brittle, box-ticking work | Work on the underlying judgment the anchor illustrates |
| Comparing yourself to the fastest engineer | Speed is not a leveling dimension and is the easiest thing to imitate | Compare scope, autonomy, and reliability instead |
| Ignoring the promotion criteria section | Your own theory of promotion rarely matches the org's | Build evidence against the written criteria and confirm them with your manager |
| Reading the ladder once at promotion time | Behavior change takes quarters; surprise gaps are the top promotion failure | Re-read the ladder every quarter and adjust growth targets |

## Success Indicators

- You can state your current level's expectation sentence and your next level's from memory
- You can name which of the five dimensions is your weakest and why
- Your manager agrees with your self-positioning on the ladder without negotiation
- You describe your work in ladder language (scope, decisions, outcomes) rather than task lists
- Your growth targets map to specific behavioral anchors, not vague wishes
