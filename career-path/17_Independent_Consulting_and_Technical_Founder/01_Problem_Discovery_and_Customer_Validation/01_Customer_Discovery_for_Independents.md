# Customer Discovery for Independents

> **Core skill:** Finding and talking to real prospects who have the problem — not friends, not a convenient sample — until the pain, its cost, and the current workaround are clear in their own words.

## Why This Matters

Employed engineers inherit their problems: a roadmap, a backlog, a manager decides what is worth building. The independent faces the open market with nothing inherited. Discovery is the first act of the business — it decides what to build, what to sell, and to whom — and mistakes here are paid for with months of unpaid work, not with a sprint of wasted velocity.

The independent's version of discovery has a different budget from a funded startup's: no research team, no panel budget, no months of runway to burn. It must be fast, cheap, and honest. It also does double duty: a well-run discovery conversation is the first sales conversation, and it either seeds trust and pipeline or burns the best prospects you have.

What counts as evidence is narrow and demanding: recurring pain described without prompting, a quantified cost, and an existing workaround or budget line the pain already touches. Chatting with anyone who is easy to reach produces comfortable conversations and worthless conclusions. The independent discovers for two outcomes at once — enough evidence to design an offer and enough relationships to launch it.

## Who Counts as a Real Prospect

| Source | Why It Tempts You | What It Can Prove |
|--------|-------------------|-------------------|
| Friends and family | Willing listeners; low rejection risk | Almost nothing — sympathy is not demand |
| Former colleagues | Shared context makes the first call easy | Real signal only if they now own the problem |
| Peer independents | Easy to reach; share your vocabulary | Market tooling gaps, not buyer pain |
| Public communities and forums | Complaints are visible and searchable | The vocabulary of the pain; weak on payment intent |
| Targeted strangers with a cold ask | High rejection; slow to schedule | The only sample that resembles the actual market |
| Inbound readers and subscribers | They came to you already | Strongest early intent; test it before it cools |

The test for every name on the list: could this person, on their own authority or with one signature, become a paying customer? If not, they are research context, not a prospect.

## The Discovery Cadence

| Step | Action | Bar to Pass |
|------|--------|-------------|
| 1 | Build a prospect list for the target role and problem | Twenty or more named people or organizations |
| 2 | Ask for fifteen minutes to learn about the problem, no pitch | Five conversations on the calendar |
| 3 | Run interviews and log facts, quotes, and numbers | Ten conversations logged in one place |
| 4 | Cluster findings and check for repeating patterns | Three or more prospects raise the same pain unprompted |
| 5 | Invite the warmest matches to a small commitment test | One commitment of money, time, or a signed pilot |

Cadence beats intensity: a standing hour per day of discovery work keeps the pipeline warm while delivery work consumes the calendar.

## The Discovery Loop

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    LIST["List real prospects"] --> ASK["Ask about the problem, not the idea"]
    ASK --> LOG["Log facts, quotes, and numbers"]
    LOG --> PATTERN["Look for repeating pain"]
    PATTERN --> INVITE["Invite a small commitment test"]
```

The loop closes when each conversation sharpens the next question, the list, and the offer hypothesis. Discovery is never a phase that ends at launch; it is a running practice that keeps the offer honest.

## Practical Applications

### Discovery Readiness Checklist

- [ ] Target role and problem space named in one sentence
- [ ] Prospect list contains twenty or more named people or organizations
- [ ] Ten interviews completed, mostly with strangers who match the target
- [ ] Facts, quotes, and numbers logged per conversation in one place
- [ ] Current workarounds and existing spend documented per prospect
- [ ] At least one commitment test offered to the warmest prospect

### Discovery Log Template

```markdown
## Discovery Log — <prospect, date>

| Field | Notes |
|-------|-------|
| Role and context | <who they are, team, tools they use> |
| Problem in their words | <verbatim quote> |
| Cost of the problem | <hours, money, risk per month> |
| Current workaround | <tools, manual steps, external spend> |
| Decision process | <who signs, triggers, budget cycle> |
| Next step agreed | <date, artifact, intro> |
```

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Is a Problem | Better Approach |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Friends as research** | Sympathy reads as demand and inflates confidence | Interview strangers who match the target; use friends only to rehearse |
| **Pitching in the conversation** | The pitch contaminates every answer that follows | Ask about their past and present; defer the solution to a later call |
| **Convenient sample** | Whoever replies is rarely who pays | Reach the buyer role, not the friendliest person in the network |
| **No numbers collected** | Vague pain cannot be sized, priced, or prioritized | Ask what the problem costs in hours, money, or failed outcomes |
| **Stopping at enthusiasm** | Interest is cheap and nearly universal | Climb to a commitment test before building anything |
| **Discovery stops at launch** | Offers drift from the market within months | Keep a standing cadence of discovery conversations every quarter |

## Success Indicators

- Prospects describe the same pain unprompted across independent conversations
- Offer drafts quote prospect language rather than your paraphrases
- The prospect list grows through referrals instead of cold effort
- A commitment test is running within weeks, not months
- Discovery conversations convert into pipeline without a hard pivot to selling

## Summary

Customer discovery is the independent's first discipline and their insurance policy: find real prospects who match the buyer role, talk to them about their actual past and present, log facts instead of impressions, and keep climbing until someone commits something scarce. Done well, it is simultaneously research, positioning, and the start of the sales pipeline — and it is the cheapest way to avoid the most expensive mistake in independent work, building something nobody will buy.
