# Project Communication Management

> **Core skill:** Designing and executing the project communication system — determining what information each stakeholder needs, when they need it, through what channel, and in what format — so communication is a deliberate system, not a stream of forwarded emails.

## Why This Matters

Communication is the project manager's most frequent activity and the one most likely to be mistaken for "just sending updates." In reality, project communication is a system: inputs (project data), processing (analysis and translation), outputs (reports, briefs, presentations), and feedback (questions, concerns, decisions). When the system works, stakeholders have the information they need to decide and act. When it fails, they decide on rumor.

The communication plan is the system's design document. It answers, for every stakeholder group: what information, in what format, at what cadence, through what channel, with what expectation of response. The plan prevents the two most common communication failures: information starvation (stakeholders lack what they need) and information flooding (stakeholders receive everything and read nothing).

## Communication Needs by Stakeholder

| Stakeholder | Needs | Format | Cadence |
|-------------|-------|--------|---------|
| Sponsor | Decisions required, top risks, schedule/cost variance | Executive brief, 1:1 conversation | Weekly |
| Steering committee | Progress against plan, key decisions, major risks | Decision brief, dashboard | Monthly |
| Project team | Task assignments, dependencies, blockers, changes | Standup, task board, team channel | Daily / continuous |
| Users | Feature progress, UAT schedule, training plan | Demo, newsletter, portal | Biweekly |
| Executives (wider) | Strategic alignment, major milestones, headline risks | One-page summary, town hall | Quarterly |
| Vendors | Contract status, delivery milestones, issues | Status report, review meeting | Weekly / milestone |
| Regulators | Compliance evidence, audit trail | Formal submission | As required |

One size does not fit all: the sponsor's weekly brief and the team's daily standup serve different needs and should not share a format.

## Communication Channels

| Channel | Strengths | Weaknesses | Best For |
|---------|-----------|------------|----------|
| **1:1 meeting** | Candor, nuance, relationship | Doesn't scale, no record | Sponsor, key stakeholders |
| **Status report** | Structured, archivable, distributable | One-way, easy to ignore | Regular updates to broad groups |
| **Dashboard** | Visual, always current, self-service | Requires discipline to maintain | Steering committee, executives |
| **Town hall** | Broad reach, interactive, visible leadership | Infrequent, shallow per topic | Organizational awareness |
| **Email / newsletter** | Push delivery, archived | Low engagement, inbox competition | Scheduled summaries |
| **Collaboration tool** | Real-time, team-owned, persistent | Noise if undisciplined | Project team coordination |
| **Decision brief** | Focused, actionable, timed for a decision | Time-consuming to prepare | Governance forums |

The channel must match the message: a decision belongs in a brief, not in a newsletter; a status update belongs on a dashboard, not in a 1:1.

## The Communication Plan

### Plan Structure

| Element | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Audience | Stakeholder group (linked to register) |
| Information need | What they need to know or decide |
| Content | Specific data, analysis, and messages |
| Format | Document, dashboard, presentation, conversation |
| Channel | How it is delivered |
| Cadence | Frequency and timing |
| Owner | Who prepares and delivers |
| Feedback mechanism | How the audience responds or asks questions |
| Success measure | How we know communication is working |

### Communication Cadence Map

```
Weekly:    Sponsor brief → Friday AM
           Team status → Friday PM
           Risk watch → Monday AM
Biweekly:  Steering committee prep → Wednesday before
           Stakeholder newsletter → Thursday
Monthly:   Steering committee meeting → First Wednesday
           Project dashboard refresh → First Monday
           Vendor review → Second week
Quarterly: Executive summary → Month 1 of quarter
           Town hall → Month 2 of quarter
           Communication plan review → Month 3 of quarter
```

## Writing Project Communications

### The Status Report

A status report is not a diary of activities — it is a structured assessment:

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Headline** | One-sentence summary: on track / at risk / off track and why |
| **This period** | What was accomplished — deliverables, milestones, decisions |
| **Next period** | What is planned — deliverables, milestones, decisions required |
| **Variance** | Schedule, cost, scope — with explanation and corrective action |
| **Risks and issues** | Top risks, new issues, resolved issues |
| **Decisions needed** | What decisions, from whom, by when |

### The Decision Brief

A decision brief presents a choice, not a lecture:

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Decision required** | What decision, from whom, by when |
| **Context** | Why this decision matters now |
| **Options** | 2-3 options with pros, cons, cost, and risk per option |
| **Recommendation** | The project manager's recommended option and rationale |
| **Impact of no decision** | What happens if the decision is deferred |

### The Executive Summary

| Section | Content |
|---------|---------|
| **Bottom line** | Overall project health — one paragraph |
| **Key accomplishments** | 3-5 bullet points |
| **Key risks** | 3 with mitigation status |
| **Next milestone** | Date and confidence |
| **Ask** | What the executive needs to do |

## Communication in Programs

Program managers layer communication:

- **Program-level**: consolidated status, cross-project risks, benefits tracking — for executives and steering committee
- **Project-level**: detailed status, team coordination — for project teams and project stakeholders
- **Integration**: the program communication plan ensures project communications are consistent in format, cadence, and message — the organization receives one program story, not N conflicting project stories

## Practical Applications

### Communication Plan Checklist

- [ ] Every stakeholder group has a defined communication need, format, channel, and cadence
- [ ] Status reports follow the structured format: headline, this period, next period, variance, risks, decisions
- [ ] Decision briefs present options and a recommendation — not just a problem
- [ ] Communication channels match message types — decisions in briefs, not newsletters
- [ ] The communication plan is reviewed quarterly for effectiveness
- [ ] Stakeholders are surveyed: do they receive the right information at the right time?

## Common Pitfalls

| Pitfall | Why It Is a Problem | Better Approach |
|---------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Activity-as-status** | "The team met three times" is not a status — it is a diary | Report accomplishments, variance, and decisions |
| **One-channel fallacy** | Everything goes to everyone through one channel | Channel matched to message and audience |
| **Information flooding** | Stakeholders receive everything and read nothing | Curate per audience; less is more |
| **No feedback loop** | Communication is one-way; issues fester unvoiced | Every communication includes a feedback mechanism |
| **Dashboard neglect** | The dashboard is built once and never updated | Dashboard discipline: refresh on cadence, or retire it |

## Success Indicators

- Stakeholders report receiving the right information at the right time
- Status reports are read and referenced in governance meetings
- Decision briefs result in timely decisions — not deferred for "more information"
- The communication plan is reviewed and improved each quarter
- No stakeholder escalates a concern that was visible in project communications

## Summary

Project communication management is the design and operation of the project's information system: tailored content per stakeholder group, delivered through matched channels at defined cadences, with structured formats for status, decisions, and executive summaries, and feedback mechanisms that make communication a loop rather than a broadcast. The project manager who treats communication as a system builds stakeholder confidence; the one who treats it as "sending updates" builds inbox noise. The communication plan is the system's blueprint — and it works when stakeholders have what they need to decide and act, and nothing they do not.
